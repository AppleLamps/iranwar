Viewing Overview
What Is This War?

The 2026 Iran War is a direct US-Israeli military conflict against Iran, launched on February 28, 2026. It is the most significant US military engagement in the Middle East since the 2003 Iraq War.

Important context: This conflict follows the June 2025 "12-Day War," in which Israel struck Iran's nuclear enrichment infrastructure and ballistic missile sites. The current war is a far larger and sustained campaign involving US forces.
Why Did the War Start?

The trigger was Iran's continued nuclear program, its ballistic missile capabilities, persistent proxy attacks across the region, and its stranglehold on the Strait of Hormuz — through which roughly 20% of global oil flows. The Trump administration, backed by Israel, decided diplomacy had failed.

Iran had also violently suppressed mass protests in December 2025 and January 2026, with an estimated 30,000 protesters killed. This weakened the regime's domestic legitimacy and signaled to the US and Israel that the regime was vulnerable.

Who Is Fighting?

Coalition: The United States and Israel conduct coordinated air strikes. The US contributes strategic bombers, carrier strike groups, A-10s, Apaches, and F-35s. Israel conducts precision decapitation and surgical strikes.

Iran & its "Axis of Resistance": Iran fires missiles and drones at Israel and Gulf states. Hezbollah (Lebanon), the Houthis (Yemen), and Iranian-backed Iraqi militias attack from multiple directions.

What Are the War's Objectives?

US/Israel goals: Destroy Iran's nuclear program and weapons production capability; eliminate Iran's ability to disrupt shipping through the Strait of Hormuz; degrade Iran's missile and drone forces; potentially "create conditions for regime change."

Iran's goals: Survive the assault while retaining its nuclear program and asserting permanent control over the Strait of Hormuz as the centerpiece of future deterrence.

What Phase Are We In? (May 19)

The intensive air campaign remains paused under an informal ceasefire, but the war has shifted into a strike-suspension window rather than a settlement. Trump canceled a planned May 19 strike on May 18 after Gulf leaders intervened, while warning US forces remain ready for a full assault if negotiations fail. The blockade remains active and the core disputes over Iran's nuclear program and Strait of Hormuz control are still unresolved.

Key Context: Before the War
The Road to February 28, 2026

June 2025 — The "12-Day War": Israel struck Iran's nuclear enrichment sites and ballistic missile infrastructure in a limited campaign. Iran retaliated but failed to stop Israel. Iran quietly began repairing and hiding its missile forces afterward.

December 2025 – January 2026 — Mass Protests: A wave of anti-regime protests erupted across Iran. The IRGC and Basij killed approximately 30,000 protesters to suppress the uprising. Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei was reportedly incapacitated and effectively died around this time.

The Succession Crisis: With Khamenei dead or dying, his son Mojtaba Khamenei was positioned as successor — a controversial move that set off fierce internal factional fighting between pragmatists and IRGC hardliners. Ali Larijani, the SNSC Secretary, was reportedly "effectively running the country" and lobbying to have a more moderate supreme leader selected.

February 28, 2026: The US and Israel launched a coordinated air campaign. The war had begun.

Tunnel Entrances & Missile Bases

The combined force struck 29+ missile launch bases and blocked access to underground launchers by collapsing tunnel entrances. CNN analyzed 107 Iranian tunnel entrances and found 77% were struck.

Iran tried to use bulldozers during a ceasefire to clear rubble from entrances. The combined force struck a bulldozer mid-operation at the Dezful Missile Base. As of April 14, US intelligence assessed roughly half of Iran's missile launchers remain operational.

Air Defenses: Functionally Defeated

Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth declared on March 13 that "Iran has no air defenses." By March 19, the IDF confirmed destruction of approximately 85% of Iran's surface-to-air missiles.

The combined force achieved near-total air superiority over large parts of Iran, enabling continued precision strikes throughout the campaign. US F-35s flew combat missions over Iran, with one struck by residual Iranian anti-aircraft fire and forced into an emergency landing.

Naval Campaign

The combined force decimated Iran's naval forces across three seas:

  • Bandar Abbas Port: IRGC Navy HQ, multiple warships struck
  • Caspian Sea (Bandar Anzali): Russia-Iran supply route severed; frigate IRIS Deylaman hit, dozens of vessels struck
  • Persian Gulf: 16 Iranian mine-laying vessels destroyed by CENTCOM
  • Kharg Island: 90+ targets including naval mine storage, missile storage, radars
Internal Security Targeting

A unique feature of this campaign: the combined force deliberately targeted Iran's internal repression machinery — Basij checkpoints, police stations, LEC logistics divisions, and IRGC patrol units — down to motorcycle units used to suppress protests.

At least 2,200 munitions struck IRGC/Basij/LEC targets. Israeli drones disrupted Basij and LEC patrols during Chaharshanbe Suri celebrations, effectively providing "an air umbrella" for Iranian civilians. Security forces began sleeping under bridges and in vehicles to avoid targeting.

Psychological impact
Nuclear Program Degraded

US and Israeli strikes in 2026 targeted Iran's nuclear weaponization infrastructure. The Institute for Science and International Security confirmed hits on six nuclear-related SPND sites covering metallurgy, explosives research, neutronics, and diagnostics.

Key scientists were killed. The tunnel complex at the Esfahan Nuclear Technology Center remains sealed with new concrete berms. No reconstruction at 2025-bombed enrichment sites has been detected. Combined effect of June 2025 and 2026 strikes: significantly increased the time and uncertainty for Iran to build a nuclear weapon.

Iran's Retaliation

Iran fought back on multiple fronts — missile barrages against Israel, drone and missile attacks on Gulf states, naval mines in the strait, attacks on US forces, and economic warfare through energy infrastructure targeting.

Scale: Iran launched hundreds of ballistic missiles and thousands of drones at Israel and Gulf states over the course of the war. The UAE alone intercepted 327 ballistic missiles, 15 cruise missiles, and 1,699 drones before the ceasefire.
Attacks on Israel

Iran launched dozens of ballistic missile salvoes at Israel throughout the war, using Khorramshahr-4, Ghadr, Emad, and Kheibar Shekan missiles — many armed with cluster munitions. Iranian cluster munitions struck central Israel multiple times, killing and injuring civilians.

The heaviest days of Iranian fire at Israel came early in the campaign (on the order of dozens to roughly 90 missiles on February 28 and about 60 on March 1, per ISW-CTP tracking). Later, Iran shifted toward smaller salvoes spread across the day; by late March the IDF described an average of about 10 missiles per day to Israel (a daily rate, not one volley). In early April one salvo included 10 missiles and was described as among the largest of that later phase. Iranian missiles killed civilians in Ramat Gan and struck near the Orot Rabin power plant and Tel Aviv's Savidor Central railway station. Israel's air defenses (Iron Dome, Arrow, David's Sling) intercepted the vast majority.

Attacks on Gulf States

Iran attacked all six Gulf Cooperation Council states simultaneously, seeking to punish them for hosting US bases and to destabilize global energy markets:

  • Qatar: Two Iranian missiles struck Ras Laffan Industrial City; 17% of LNG export capacity destroyed, repairs expected to take up to 5 years
  • UAE: Largest target — 327 BMs, 15 CMs, 1,699 drones intercepted. 2 Emirati soldiers and 6 civilians killed
  • Saudi Arabia: Prince Sultan Air Base hit, 5 US KC-135 tankers damaged; SAMREF Refinery drone struck
  • Kuwait: Fuel tanks at Kuwait Airport struck by drone; two oil refineries hit
  • Bahrain: Citibank building struck; telecommunications company building targeted
Maritime Warfare

Iran deployed naval mines in the Strait of Hormuz (fewer than 10), severely chilling commercial shipping. An Iranian unmanned surface vessel struck the Egyptian-Maltese container ship Safeen Prestige. Iran struck the Qatari state-leased tanker AQUA 1 northwest of Ras Laffan.

Traffic through the Strait collapsed from ~138 vessels per day to just ~2 per day at the peak of the conflict. Iran established an "Iranian-approved route" requiring IRGC coordination — effectively asserting toll-road sovereignty over an international waterway.

Attacks on US Forces

Iranian-backed Iraqi militias conducted hundreds of attacks against US forces and diplomatic facilities, including:

  • Repeated drone strikes on the US Embassy in Baghdad
  • Attacks on Erbil International Airport and US consulate
  • Drone ambush of US Embassy personnel near Baghdad Airport (FPV drones, April 8)
  • Attacks on US bases in Syria (Qasrak Base, Hasakah)
  • Strikes on UK-owned energy infrastructure in Erbil

Energy Infrastructure Targeting (Iran vs. Gulf)
Iran Targets the Gulf Economy

Iran deliberately attacked Gulf energy infrastructure to impose economic costs and coerce Gulf states to pressure the US and Israel to halt strikes. Targets included Ras Laffan LNG (Qatar), SAMREF Refinery (Saudi Arabia), Emirates Global Aluminum (UAE), ALBA Aluminum (Bahrain), Kuwait oil refineries, and the Fujairah export terminal (UAE).

In response to combined force strikes on Iranian steel factories, Iran struck Gulf aluminum facilities — explicit "tit-for-tat" economic warfare targeting industrial supply chains.

The Strait of Hormuz — Iran's Ultimate Card

The Strait of Hormuz is the single most important piece of the entire conflict. Understanding Iran's strategy here is key to understanding everything.

Why it matters: Roughly 20% of global oil — and 30% of global LNG — passes through the Strait of Hormuz. An Iranian advisor to the Supreme Leader compared Iran's control over the strait to "the strategic value of a nuclear weapon."
Iran's Strategy

Iran does not need to sink ships to control the strait. Simply threatening to do so is enough to crater global shipping confidence. Iran established an "Iranian-approved route" requiring vessel operators to contact IRGC intermediaries before transiting — a de facto toll system on an international waterway.

Iran allows "friendly" vessels (Chinese, Greek, Iranian, Pakistani) to transit while blocking vessels from the US, Israel, and allied nations. By March 25, at least 26 vessels were using the Iranian-approved route, and 92% of strait traffic was Iranian, Greek, or Chinese.

Why This Is the Core Dispute

Iran views the strait as its replacement deterrent. Having lost 85% of its air defenses, most of its missile production facilities, and much of its proxy infrastructure, the IRGC calculates that the ability to shut down 20% of global oil at any moment is Iran's best long-term security guarantee.

Senior IRGC Commander Ahmad Vahidi and hardliners are reportedly willing to continue the war rather than cede "sovereignty" over the strait. Iranian Parliament is drafting a bill to formally codify Iranian control into law.

US Response — Naval Blockade

On April 13, the US imposed a full naval blockade of Iranian ports. CENTCOM announced it would prevent vessels from entering or exiting Iranian ports and that all Iranian vessels and sanctioned vessels are subject to stop and search anywhere in international waters.

By May 18, CENTCOM said it had redirected 85 commercial vessels and disabled four since the blockade began on April 13. The blockade is still trapping a large volume of Iranian exports and preserving US leverage while negotiations remain unresolved.

The Impasse

The US demands Iran fully reopen the strait before negotiations proceed. Iran demands the US end all attacks and the naval blockade before it will discuss the strait. Iran's latest proposal calls for discussing "Tehran's conditions for opening the Strait of Hormuz" simultaneously with US guarantees to end attacks.

On May 18, Trump suspended a planned May 19 strike after Qatar, Saudi Arabia, and the UAE asked for a short pause, but he also ordered US forces to stay ready for a large-scale assault at short notice if talks fail. Iran's latest counterproposal still does not meet US demands on enrichment and highly enriched uranium.

The Multi-Front Proxy War

Iran activated its entire "Axis of Resistance" network — Hezbollah in Lebanon, Houthis in Yemen, and Iraqi militias — opening multiple fronts against the US and Israel simultaneously.

🇱🇧 Hezbollah (Lebanon)

Hezbollah formally joined the war on March 1–2, launching rockets at northern Israel. By late March, Hezbollah was conducting up to 79 attacks per day — its highest ever. The IDF reported 1,400+ Hezbollah fighters killed in Lebanon since the start of its campaign on March 2 (per ISW-CTP reporting, April 10, 2026).

Weapons used: Rockets, anti-tank guided missiles (ATGMs) against Merkava tanks, Merkava tank engagements, drones, and increasingly — fiber-optic FPV drones (immune to jamming). Hezbollah flew FPV drones near Israeli forces 10+ times per day by late April.

Key development: Hezbollah launched "Operation Khyber 1" (March 17), fired a Paveh long-range cruise missile targeting northern Israel (April 14), and by late April was regularly striking IDF vehicles with FPV drones in southern Lebanon.

🇾🇪 Houthis (Yemen)

The Houthis entered the conflict on March 28, 2026 — launching ballistic missiles at southern Israel. By April 1 they had attacked four times, coordinating with Iran and Hezbollah in a stated unified strike campaign.

US forces have continued striking Houthi targets in Yemen throughout the conflict. The Houthis' continued ability to fire long-range ballistic missiles at Israel (~2,000km range) stretches Israeli air defenses and forces Israel to keep interceptors in reserve.

🇮🇶 Iraqi Militias

Iranian-backed Iraqi militias (Kataib Hezbollah, Asaib Ahl al Haq, Badr Organization, PMF brigades) conducted hundreds of drone and rocket attacks during the war. Key targets: US Embassy Baghdad, Erbil International Airport, US bases in Syria.

FPV drone escalation: Fiber-optic FPV drones were used to conduct a drone ambush of US Embassy personnel on April 8 and attack Kuwaiti border posts in April. An IRGC officer was arrested by Iraqi authorities for directing drone attacks on Baghdad Airport — confirming direct IRGC command of these operations.

Iranian-backed militias also attacked British energy infrastructure in Iraqi Kurdistan, a French base (killing one French soldier), and UAE/Saudi consulates in Erbil.

  • Abu Hussein al Hamidawi (Kataib Hezbollah head) targeted in Baghdad

FPV Drone Proliferation — The New Threat

One of the most significant new developments in this war is the widespread use of fiber-optic first-person view (FPV) drones — drones guided by a physical fiber-optic cable rather than radio signals, making them completely immune to electronic jamming. Russia pioneered these in Ukraine and appears to have transferred the technology to Iran, which has disseminated it to Hezbollah and Iraqi militias.

Hezbollah is flying FPV drones at Israeli forces 10+ times per day in southern Lebanon. Israel has shot down at least 27 FPV drones with Iron Dome as of May 1, 2026 (Israeli media, via ISW-CTP), but has not found a reliable counter-system. PM Netanyahu acknowledged it "will take time" to solve the FPV threat. The confidential Russian proposal to give Iran 5,000 such drones would dramatically escalate this threat across the entire Axis of Resistance network.

Strategic note: The killing of Ali Khamenei (Supreme Leader) created a succession crisis, with his son Mojtaba attempting to consolidate power while the IRGC seized control of decision-making. This internal fragmentation is a primary reason why Iran cannot negotiate with a unified voice.

Psychological Impact of Decapitation Strikes

The effect of Israeli strikes on Iran's internal security apparatus has been documented across multiple reporting sources:

  • Security forces abandoned established headquarters and set up improvised tent-based command posts
  • Police stations across Tehran were abandoned by rank-and-file officers
  • Forces hid under bridges, in vehicles, mosques, and sports facilities to avoid aerial surveillance
  • The Basij Organization Commander was reportedly operating from a tent in a wooded area before being killed
  • Israel deployed individual phone calls to Iranian commanders, warning them to stand aside
  • Tips from ordinary Iranian civilians helped Israeli intelligence locate security personnel
  • Qatar's state media reported civilian Iranians celebrating strikes that killed protest-suppression forces

Energy Infrastructure Damage

The combined force struck Iran's most critical energy assets. Israel attacked the South Pars natural gas field (the world's largest, shared with Qatar) on March 18, damaging up to one-fifth of Iran's gas processing capacity.

Iranian gas flows to Iraq stopped completely following the strikes, depriving Iraq of over one-third of its gas and electricity. Oil terminal infrastructure on Kharg Island, Lavan Island, and at multiple refineries was also struck. Iran's shadow oil fleet has been disrupted by the US naval blockade.

Banking System Crisis

Iranian Parliament Budget Committee member Mehrdad Lahouti warned in April that Iranian banks are "in poor condition" and risk "crisis or bankruptcy." Production has "practically stalled," exporters have halted activity, and hotel closures in Tehran are spreading.

The US Treasury designated 35+ entities in Iran's "shadow banking" network (worth tens of billions annually) and three additional banking networks in May. The US warned banks in Oman, UAE, Hong Kong, and China against facilitating Iranian funds.

Industrial Strikes

The combined force targeted Iran's steel industry — key both for the economy and for missile manufacturing:

  • Mobarakeh Steel Company (Esfahan) — struck twice
  • Khuzestan Steel Company (Ahvaz) — struck twice
  • Sefid Dasht Industrial Zone (Boroujen) — struck

Combined with missile production facility destruction, Iran's defense industrial base has been severely degraded across multiple key sectors.

Political Consequences

President Masoud Pezeshkian warned in late March that the IRGC's actions were pushing the economy toward collapse within 3–4 weeks if no ceasefire was reached. The IRGC dismissed his concerns and sidelined his government entirely.

A "military council" of senior IRGC officers has assumed control of the regime's core decision-making apparatus and has established a security perimeter around Supreme Leader Mojtaba Khamenei — blocking even the president from getting a meeting. Iran's rial hit its all-time low (1.8 million per dollar) by April 29.

Diplomacy & Negotiations

Multiple rounds of diplomacy — directly between US and Iran, and on the Lebanon front — have produced ceasefires but no lasting agreement. The core issues remain unresolved.

US 15-Point Proposal (March 24)

The US delivered a 15-point proposal to Iran via Pakistani Army Commander Asim Munir, who has served as the key intermediary. The proposal required:

  • Dismantlement of Iran's nuclear program
  • End to uranium enrichment
  • Handover of Iran's highly enriched uranium (HEU) stockpile
  • Full IAEA access to all nuclear facilities
  • Limits on Iran's missile capabilities
  • Cessation of support for the Axis of Resistance (Hezbollah, Houthis, Iraqi militias)
  • Freedom of navigation through the Strait of Hormuz
Iran: Counterproposals rejected
Iran's Counter-Demands

Iran proposed five conditions for a ceasefire and has continued to signal inflexibility on core issues:

  • Complete cessation of all US and Israeli attacks
  • Mechanism to prevent renewed conflict (effectively a guarantee the US/Israel will never attack again)
  • Compensation for wartime damages
  • End to attacks on the Axis of Resistance
  • International recognition of Iran's "authority" over the Strait of Hormuz
  • Nuclear discussions postponed to a "later stage" — the nuclear file is "closed" for now
Islamabad Talks (April 11–12)

US VP JD Vance, Jared Kushner, and Steve Witkoff met with Iranian Parliament Speaker Mohammad Bagher Ghalibaf and Foreign Minister Abbas Araghchi in Islamabad, Pakistan.

The talks lasted 21 hours before collapsing. Hardliner SNSC Secretary Zolghadr was reportedly furious that Araghchi appeared open to compromising on Iran's support for the Axis of Resistance. Zolghadr alerted senior IRGC leaders, who ordered the delegation home.

US officials later said: "we aren't sure who's in charge and neither do they."

Iran's Fragmented Negotiating Team

The fundamental problem: Iran does not have a single negotiating authority. Four competing power centers each hold different red lines:

  • IRGC Cmdr. Ahmad Vahidi — holds ultimate veto power; refuses to discuss missiles; opposes concessions; only official with access to Supreme Leader
  • Parliament Speaker Ghalibaf — led delegation; pragmatist; publicly supports negotiations but lacks military command authority
  • FM Abbas Araghchi — most flexible; faces protest from IRGC affiliates outside the Foreign Ministry
  • SNSC Sec. Zolghadr — hardline IRGC veteran; was furious at any Axis of Resistance concession; called delegation home
Israel-Lebanon Diplomacy

Parallel to the Iran negotiations, the US has brokered direct talks between Israel and Lebanon — the first direct talks between the two countries in over 30 years. The meetings happened on April 14 in Washington, DC, with a second round on April 23. A temporary 10-day ceasefire in Lebanon took effect April 16 and has been extended.

Lebanon's position: LAF to deploy south, Israeli withdrawal from Lebanese territory, no Hezbollah representative on the delegation. France has proposed a four-stage peace plan that would result in Lebanon normalizing relations with Israel. Hezbollah is attempting to pressure US and Israel to extend the ceasefire further while maintaining forces "with fingers on the trigger."

Current Situation — May 19, 2026

Where things stand right now across every dimension of the conflict.

No settlement yet. Trump canceled the planned May 19 strike on May 18 after Gulf leaders asked for time, but he kept US forces ready for a full-scale assault if talks fail. Iran's latest counterproposal still falls short of US demands on enrichment and highly enriched uranium.
Military Situation
Ceasefire (partial)

The active air campaign against Iran remains paused under an informal ceasefire with no written settlement. Trump halted the planned May 19 strike, but only temporarily: he said US forces are still prepared to launch a full, large-scale assault on short notice if diplomacy breaks down.

The US naval blockade of Iranian ports remains in full effect. CENTCOM said on May 18 that US forces had redirected 85 commercial vessels and disabled four since the blockade began.

Lebanon
10-day ceasefire extended

Israel-Lebanon ceasefire has been in effect since April 16, but it remains tense. Israeli officials are still weighing broader action against Hezbollah's FPV drone network as strikes and drone attacks continue under the ceasefire's loose framework.

After Israel killed Hezbollah Radwan commander Ahmad Ghaleb "Malik" Balout on May 6, the IDF kept expanding strikes in Lebanon. On May 18, Israel announced that a May 17 strike near Baalbek killed PIJ Bekaa Valley operations commander Wael Mahmoud Abdel Halim.

Negotiations
Stalled

The US and Iran are still pursuing fundamentally incompatible negotiating positions. The latest Iranian counterproposal does not commit to suspending enrichment, transferring highly enriched uranium, or accepting the broader dismantlement terms Washington wants.

Trump's May 18 pause bought mediators only a short window. Pakistan and Gulf states are still trying to keep talks alive, but the basic dispute over nuclear concessions and Hormuz control has not narrowed.

Iran's Internal Situation
IRGC in control

IRGC hardliners still appear to dominate strategic decision-making, while civilian officials remain sidelined. The leadership is trying to negotiate from a position of strength even though the regime is also rebuilding damaged internal security structures and managing economic strain.

Iran continues acting as though control of Hormuz can replace the military and political leverage it lost earlier in the war, which helps explain why it is staying rigid on both the strait and nuclear issues.

Gulf States Under Pressure
Active targeting

Likely Iranian or Iranian-backed forces launched three drones at the Barakah Nuclear Power Plant in Abu Dhabi on May 17, and likely Iranian-backed Iraqi militias launched three drones toward Saudi Arabia the same day.

Those attacks help explain why Qatar, Saudi Arabia, and the UAE pressed Trump to suspend the planned May 19 strike: they feared Iran would retaliate directly against Gulf energy and oil infrastructure.

Hormuz Coercion
New threat

Iran is trying to turn temporary wartime disruption into a formalized Strait of Hormuz regime. On May 18, the Persian Gulf Strait Authority claimed legal control over transit and warned that passage without coordination would be illegal.

Iranian and IRGC-linked outlets are now threatening fees, permit systems, and even undersea cable oversight as part of a broader push to make Iranian approval the price of normal commerce through the strait.

Key Questions Going Forward
  • Will Iran accept a deal, or will the paused May 19 strike simply be delayed?
  • Can Iran's fragmented leadership — with Vahidi as de facto ruler — produce a coherent negotiating position?
  • Will the naval blockade force Iran's economy to collapse fast enough to change IRGC calculations?
  • Will the Israel-Lebanon ceasefire hold, or will Hezbollah's FPV drone campaign escalate into a renewed ground operation?
  • Will Russia actually supply Iran with 5,000 fiber-optic drones, and how would that change the military balance?
  • Is Mojtaba Khamenei capable of governing, and can he arbitrate between the IRGC and pragmatist factions?
  • Will economic collapse inside Iran trigger the protests the IRGC fears — and what would that mean for the regime?
